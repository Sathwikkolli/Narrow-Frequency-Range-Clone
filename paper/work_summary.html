Narrow frequency band data creation

NFR methods (F1–F5)

All five keep 300 Hz – 3.4 kHz. F0 is the control: the source scaled by the same constant −6 dB headroom the five methods use.

F1 · Parks–McClellan (equiripple FIR)

Chooses coefficients to minimise the worst error anywhere in the spectrum, so leftover error is spread evenly. Sharpest, cleanest cut for a given cost. Default when quality matters.

F2 · Kaiser window FIR

Truncates the ideal infinite filter and tapers it so the edges do not ring. A bit more compute than F1 for the same quality, but a closed-form design that cannot fail. Good for automatically generated filters.

F3 · Elliptic IIR

Feeds past outputs back, so it is about eight times cheaper than the FIR methods. Used for live processing on phones and small chips. Trade-off: frequencies are delayed by different amounts, smearing timing near the band edges.

F4 · Polyphase decimation

Lowers the sampling rate instead of filtering, so content above the new Nyquist disappears. Fastest and shrinks the file. If anti-aliasing is weak, high frequencies fold back into the kept band.

F5 · FFT overlap-add

The same filter as F2, applied in blocks in the frequency domain. Identical output, about ten times faster once the filter is long.

Why constant-Q spectrograms

The first spectrogram used a fixed 2048-sample window (43 ms at 48 kHz, hop 256). That gives about 23 Hz resolution everywhere, too coarse for the speaker's F0 (about 100–140 Hz), so F0 looked blurred. The switch to constant-Q (Q = 18, 48 bins per octave, 50 Hz – 20 kHz, hop 240 samples = 5 ms) gives a window of about 150 ms at 120 Hz and 4.5 ms at 4 kHz, so F0 and its harmonics appear as clear lines while high-frequency timing stays sharp.

Telephony simulation with ITU-T G.191

Chosen because it is the official tool set phone companies use to test call quality, so the conditions follow a real standard. It is free, runs on Great Lakes and gives the same output every time.

FF Trump real and fake audio→stereo to mono, 16 kHz→downsample to 8 kHz→telephone band shaping (IRS8)→level normalisation (ITU-T P.56)→save narrowband clip→constant-Q spectrogram

Three conditions, each changing one thing so the cause of any change can be identified:

  • P0: control, level normalisation only (ITU-T P.56, −26 dBov), 16 kHz
  • B1: narrowband telephone, 8 kHz, 300–3400 Hz via the IRS handset response (ITU-T P.48)
  • C1: B1 plus the G.711 µ-law codec, matching a landline call

RAW in the tables means the untouched original audio.

ASVspoof 2019 LA: RawNet2 goes from 4.42% (F0) to 24–26% under F1–F5 and about 35.7% under B1/C1. LCNN goes to about 18.3%, and the codec alone pushes it to 53.65% (C1). AASIST stays at or below 0.32%. Nes2Net reaches 0.86–1.21%; TCM and SLS reach 2.56–3.53%. Level normalisation alone (RAW → P0) moves RawNet2 from 4.60% to 6.14%, so ITU conditions should be compared against P0.

In-the-Wild: the models that are best in-domain lose the most. Against F0, Nes2Net 13.96 → 21.79–24.11%, TCM 11.34 → 30.01–33.08%, SLS 10.16 → 22.96–25.49%, AASIST 13.83 → 16.16–17.10%. RawNet2 is near chance in every condition. B1/C1 hurt TCM and SLS less than F1–F5.

Famous Figures: every model is already near chance on unprocessed audio (28–59%). RawNet2 is the exception that improves under F1–F5 (44.89 → about 28.5%).

Choice of method: the five methods give close EERs; the widest spread is 2.19 points (RawNet2, ASVspoof) and 3.07 points (TCM, In-the-Wild).
